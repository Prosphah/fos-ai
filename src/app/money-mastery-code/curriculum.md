# CODE 2: MONEY MANAGEMENT

## Rule 101: 10% of Everything You Earn Is Yours to Keep!

It's interesting to see that you keep paying everyone else except yourself. You pay for data, you pay for food, you pay for transport, you pay for light, you pay for clothes, etc.

We pay everybody else except ourselves. So the first rule to learn as you begin your journey to financial freedom is:

**10% OF EVERYTHING YOU EARN IS YOURS TO KEEP.**

Every other expense must only come from the remaining 90%.

### Illustration
If you receive NGN 100,000 as payment for your service or product (personal income here), as a beginner, you can pay yourself NGN 10,000 (10%) immediately.

- Every single payment to yourself becomes your seed for wealth multiplication, so you don't want to joke with it.
- Imagine how angry you feel when you don't get paid after doing a job… That's how you should feel when you don't pay yourself.
- You can increase the amount you pay yourself as you gain more discipline and develop your financial muscles.

## Debt Repayment Framework

There are two types of debt: **good debt** and **bad debt**. This section is focused on how to get out of bad debt.

### 1. Identify and Isolate

The first thing you want to do when you're in debt is to identify the blood-sucking debts, meaning the debt with the highest interest rate.

**Illustration:** You owe two people. The first you owe NGN 100,000 at a 5% compounding interest rate. The second you owe NGN 50,000 at 10% compounding interest. You owe both for 5 months before you repay. This is how much you'll be repaying:

- ₦100,000 → ₦102,096.08
- ₦50,000 → ₦52,122.08

The difference may not seem like much now, but try calculating it for ₦1,000,000:

- At 10% → ₦1,042,440
- At 5% → ₦1,020,960

So the first step is to **identify and isolate** the debts with the highest interest rates, even if it's the smallest.

### 2. Divide and Conquer

Now that we know the blood-sucking debts, the next step is to reach out to the person (if it's someone you can meet) and inform them that you've decided to repay them. However, you don't have the bulk to repay at once, so you are going to pay a split amount, gradually, over a period of time (**divide**).

**Illustration:** You owe NGN 100,000 and your monthly income is NGN 100,000 as well. You can split the debt into 10 or 5 chunks to pay over 10 or 5 months. So every month, immediately you receive your salary, your first priority is to send out the 10k or 20k directly to the person (or bank) to settle the loan (**conquer**).

- Since you're currently owing debts, you're on the "red" side of the balance sheet, and until you clear your debtors you will have to pause your monthly payment to yourself (remember the first rule) if you can't spare more than 10k per month.
- **Or** you can increase your monthly payment to 20%. While you use 10% to clear your debt, the other 10% serves as your investment seed.

From experience, if you reach out to the people you're owing and speak to them as a fellow human being, they will appreciate it and most likely accept your arrangement. This is always better than ghosting the person and diminishing all the goodwill they have toward you.

When you have cleared every debt, the next thing is to **avoid falling back into debt**, and that brings us to the next point…

### Flee From Debt

Flee from anything and everything that even remotely looks like debt.

- Don't take debts from banks

- Don't take debts from your friends

## Anti-Impulse Spending Blueprint

Causes of impulse spending:
- Societal pressure
- Peer pressure
- Ego
- Scarcity mentality
- Lack of budget or financial plan

One single truth you need to know is that the wealthy know how to control their spending impulses, and that's precisely why they're wealthy.

You've probably fantasized about all the things you'd buy if you had Dangote's kind of wealth… Well, Dangote has his kind of wealth and yet he's not getting all those things. Shouldn't that tell you something?

### Illustration
An online external source estimates that Dangote has around US $700–800 million in liquid cash. His daily expenses on an average day (a very rough estimate, not factually proven) is $200K–$500K. That's an average of $350K daily.

In more relatable terms: if Dangote had total liquid cash of NGN 500K only, his daily expenses would be about ₦250!

**The single most effective way to curb your spending impulses is to never buy things instantly** (except it's your bathing soap, of course).

If you see something you "feel" you need/want, wait for **2 days and 12 hours**. If after that you still feel like you need it, it's probably a genuine need/want. From my experience, however, the "feeling" fades fast!

## Budgeting

Budgeting is a process of allocating your income to very precise uses and purposes over a fixed period of time. It simply means you will spend XX amount of money on a particular thing (say food) for a fixed period of time (1 month).

Even though it requires a lot of personal discipline and self-control to keep, budgeting keeps you from overspending and spending impulsively on a single thing or group of things.

**Tool:** Money Manager app. Transactions → Total → Budget setting → Add budget

### Illustration
You make ₦500k every month. First things first, we take out our 10% payment (₦50k), and now we have ₦450k. The basic expenses are transportation, food, toiletries, bills, airtime/data, gifts. Everyone will have a few more, but these are quite common.

| Transportation | ₦50k | ₦400k |
| Food | ₦100k | ₦300k |
| Toiletries | ₦15k | ₦285k |

Your basic needs are sorted out with some cash to spare. As a rules player, the idea is that your budget is not fixed in stone. You can adapt it to suit your needs for a certain period.

## Rule 102: Keep Track of Your Finances

A daily activity the wealthy do is recording their money. We record every single penny we make, and every single penny we spend.

Money is very jealous, so you don't want to neglect it. Have an idea of how much you make in a day, a week, a month, and a year. Also know how much you spend in a day, a week, a month, and a year. The only way you can find money leaks in your financial life is if you keep track of your money.

If you were recording your finances you would be surprised how much you spend on data, or meat pie, or Coke, and so on.

**Benefits**
- Gives you a clear idea of the spending impulses you need to cut back on
- Makes it easier to plan ahead

## Definition of Terms

- **Capital:** The amount of money you put into any investment.
- **Asset:** Anything of value you own that can generate income (e.g., stocks, land, property, bonds).
- **Capital gain:** The profit you earn when you sell an investment for a higher price than you got it.
- **Inflation:** The rise in prices of goods and services over time, which reduces the purchasing power of money.
- **Volatility:** How much and how fast the prices of investments go up and down. Rapid price changes in the market.
- **Interest:** The amount of profit made from an investment with fixed rates. Example: you deposit 100k in an investment with a fixed return of 18% per annum; at the end of the first year your investment will be NGN 118,000. NGN 18,000 is the interest of your investment.
- **Return on Investment (ROI):** The profit (or loss) you make compared to the money you invested.
- **Risk:** The chance that your investment will lose money or not perform as expected.
- **Portfolio:** A collection of all your investments.
- **Diversification:** Spreading your investments across various investment vehicles to reduce risk.
- **Liquidity:** The ease of buying and selling assets without losing much value.
- **Leverage:** Using borrowed money to invest (it can multiply profits but also losses).
- **Yield:** The income you earn from an investment.

## Categories of Investment

Investments can be categorised based on their risk level and the duration of the investment.

**Based on risk:** Low risk · Medium risk · High risk · Greed

**Based on duration (investment goals):**
- **Short term (1–12 months):** Usually lower risk, quick returns and liquidity
- **Mid term (1–3 years):** Balances moderate risk and growth
- **Long term (3 years and above):** For building wealth, retirement plans, generational assets

## Investment Vehicle 1: Bonds

When the government borrows some money from you to pay back at a particular date with interest. It's a loan you give to the government.

**Key features**
- **Issuer:** The borrower (e.g., Government of Nigeria, U.S. Treasury)
- **Principal/face value:** The amount you loan
- **Coupon rate:** The interest rate the bond pays
- **Maturity date:** When the bond "expires" and the issuer pays back your principal

It runs for 2–5 years. There are also those that last longer, so it's a long-term investment. The interest on a government bond is paid quarterly (every 3 months), and at the end of the maturity period your whole principal is given back to you.

**Apps:** Bamboo, Stanbic IBTC

## Investment Vehicle 2: T-Bills

Also a government-issued security. You're basically buying a "short-term bond" at a discount price.

**Example:** You want to invest 100k for 91 days at an 18% interest rate. 18% of 100k is NGN 18k, so what you will pay is NGN 82k. At the maturity date (91 days), you get back NGN 100k, which is your face value/principal.

**Duration:** Short term
- 91 days → 18%
- 182 days → 19%
- 364 days → 22%

**How can you get it?**
- **Primary market, CBN:** You need a minimum of NGN 50 million
- **Secondary market, apps and platforms:** Minimum of NGN 10k on Bamboo

**Apps:** Bamboo

## Investment Vehicle 4: Mutual Funds

Two common types:
- **Equity mutual fund:** High risk, high reward
- **Money market mutual fund:** Short-term investment in fixed assets like fixed deposits and T-bills. Low risk.

### How to Value a Stock
- **Market cap:** The company's size and market value.
- **Earnings Per Share (EPS):** How much profit a company makes for each share. If a company's total profit was divided equally across all shares, how much would each share get? **EPS = Profit / Number of shares**

**Why it matters**
1. The higher the EPS, the more money the company is making per share.
2. Investors use it to judge how profitable a company is compared to others.
3. It's also used to calculate the P/E ratio, which helps tell if a stock is cheap or expensive.

- **P/E ratio (price to earnings):** Stock price / EPS
  - P/E of 8–10 (low): The stock might be undervalued, or the company's growth is slowing down
  - P/E of 30–50 (high): The stock might be expensive and investors expect massive future growth
- **Revenue growth**
- **Industry trends**
- **Dividend yield:** How much cash a company pays you each year in dividends compared to its stock price. **Yield = (Dividend per share / Stock price) × 100**
  - 0–2% → Low
  - 2–4% → Balanced
  - 5% and above → High
- **Quarterly performance**

### How to Buy Stocks

- **Dollar cost averaging (DCA):** Helps you cut losses and accumulate more shares at a cheaper rate by investing a fixed amount of money at regular intervals, whether prices go up or down.

**Why it's important**
- **Reduces risk:** You're not betting everything at one price
- **Removes emotion:** You invest consistently, not based on fear or hype
- **Good for beginners:** Simple, disciplined, and automatic
- **Builds wealth over time:** Ideal for long-term goals like retirement

**Things to note**
- Works best in long-term investing (5+ years)
- Not ideal for short-term trading or lump-sum investing when prices are obviously low
- Still needs good-quality investments: DCA doesn't fix a bad stock

**Order types**

- **Limit order:** An order to buy below the current price
- **Stop order:** An order to buy a stock above the current market price, a way to catch a stock when its price is going up. Or to sell a stock when it goes below the current market price, used as a risk management tool to lock in profits.

## Hedging

A risk management strategy employed to protect an individual's finances and/or assets from risky situations and uncertainties that may result in loss (e.g., inflation).

You can't control how a market moves… but you can control how exposed you are to risk.

**Hedging instruments**
- **Forward contracts:** A private agreement to buy or sell an asset at a specific price on a specific future date. *E.g., In 3 months' time, I'll sell 3 tubers of yam at ₦5,000/tuber.*
- **Futures contracts:** Same idea as a forward contract, but traded on an exchange. *E.g., You could sell yam futures on the exchange for delivery in 3 months. If the price of yam falls, you gain. If it rises, you lose.*
- **Options:** The holder has the right to buy or sell an asset at a specific price within a certain period. You are required to pay a premium to get an option. Two types:
  - **Put option** (right to sell)
  - **Call option** (right to buy)
  *E.g., A yam farmer buys a put option that gives him the right to sell at ₦5,000/tuber. If the price of yam falls below ₦5,000, he can use his option, sell at ₦5,000 and avoid a loss. If the price rises above ₦5,000, he can ignore his option and sell at the higher price.*
- **Swaps:** An agreement between two parties to exchange cash flows over time. *E.g., Your company buys a huge amount of fuel every month for operations. You can go to the bank and set up a commodity swap: if fuel prices rise above ₦1,000/litre, you'll pay me; if fuel prices fall below ₦1,000/litre, I'll pay you.*
